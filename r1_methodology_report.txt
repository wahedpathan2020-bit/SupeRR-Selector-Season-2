2. How pressure is defined
Pressure is not one stat (required rate, wickets, balls left) but the amount the match is on the line on that ball, which
those factors jointly determine. I built it in three steps:
(a) Win-probability model. For every delivery in 766 completed IPL and T20I matches (2022 onward; no-results, ties,
rain-revised and Emerging/A-team games excluded) I trained separate gradient-boosting models for innings 1 and 2. Inputs
are balls left, wickets lost, runs, current rate, league (IPL/T20I) and, in chases, runs needed, required rate and the gap
between required and current rate. Predictions are strictly out-of-fold (5 folds by match), so no match is scored by a model
that saw it. Chase AUC is 0.89 and calibration is close to the diagonal (Figure 1); the innings-1 model is weaker (AUC 0.67),
as expected without team strength.

=== PAGE 2 ===
(b) Leverage. For each ball I compute the expected absolute change in win probability over the possible outcomes (wicket,
0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, wide), weighted by their frequency in that phase of the innings. Leverage is high when the contest is tight and
few balls remain (a chase needing 12 off 6, or 3 down in the death) and low when the game is effectively decided.
(c) High-pressure flag. A delivery is "high pressure" when leverage is in the top quartile (≥2.47 win-pts per ball), the contest
is still live (win probability 20-80%), and at least 3 overs have been bowled (this removes an early-innings model artefact).
This gives 28,264 deliveries (60% in chases) spread across 758 of 766 matches. "Low pressure" is the bottom half by
leverage and serves as each player's own baseline.
Does it separate pressure from ordinary play? Yes: mean absolute win-probability swing per ball is about 3x higher in the
flagged balls (Figure 1, right), and the flagged states are tighter and later. Scoring rate and wicket rate differ only modestly
between the two groups, which is why a pure strike-rate view of "pressure" misses much of what matters.

3. How performance is measured
Batting and bowling use the same currency, win probability added (WPA): the change in the batting side's win probability
on each ball, credited to the batter and debited from the bowler. Because the model is calibrated, an average player earns
about zero, so WPA already reads as "better than average in this exact situation" and automatically credits a dot ball at 14
an over more than one in a dead game. Non-striker run-outs and run-outs are excluded from the affected player's sample;
wides count against the bowler only.
Three components per player (all computed on high-pressure deliveries unless stated):
Impact (50%) - opponent-adjusted WPA per 100 pressure deliveries. A joint ridge regression puts every batter and bowler in
one model (batter +, bowler -), so a batter is credited for beating good bowlers and a bowler for tough batters. The ridge
penalty (α chosen by match-grouped cross-validation) is a built-in empirical-Bayes shrinkage: 8 great balls cannot outrank
300 very good ones.
Resilience (25%) - the player's own baseline test. For each player I estimate the share of the available win-probability swing
(leverage) captured under pressure and in low-pressure play, each shrunk by ridge, and take the difference. It rewards
players whose output holds or rises when the game tightens, not just players who are good all the time.
Consistency (25%) - the Beta-shrunk percentage of a player's pressure innings/spells (at least 4 pressure deliveries) in
which they added, rather than lost, win probability. This penalises one heroic knock surrounded by failures.
Composite = 0.5·z(Impact) + 0.25·z(Resilience) + 0.25·z(Consistency), z-scored among eligible players, separately for
batters and bowlers.
4. Fair comparisons
Eligibility: at least 80 high-pressure deliveries, 12 matches with a pressure delivery and 8 qualifying pressure innings/spells
(105 batters and 111 bowlers qualify). Sample size: ridge shrinkage plus Beta shrinkage on consistency, with bootstrap
uncertainty reported below. IPL vs T20I: the win-probability model has a league term so a given state is judged against its
own league's scoring; the joint adjustment credits players for the quality of opponents faced, which partly bridges IPL and
T20I because many players appear in both. Players with all of their data in one competition are flagged in the appendix (IPL
share of pressure balls) and carry more transfer risk. Match context: states, not labels, drive pressure, so a final and a
league game are judged by the same definition.

6. Reliability and limitations
Weight sensitivity. Across nine weight schemes (including impact-only, no-consistency and equal weights) the batter top
10 keeps 8-10 of the same names and the bowler top 10 keeps 7-10; rank correlation with the base scores is at least 0.95.
Redefining pressure at the top 30% or top 20% of leverage retains 9 of 10 batters and 8 of 10 bowlers.
Bootstrap. Resampling matches 60 times gives each player's probability of staying in the top 10 (Figures 2-3). The leaders
(Buttler, Tim David, Klaasen, Salt; Axar, Narine, Krunal) stay in the top 10 in roughly 70-90% of resamples; ranks 8-10 are
close calls and should be read as a tier rather than a strict order.
Honest check on persistence. Splitting matches into odd and even halves and correlating each player's ratings gives a
rank correlation of about 0.17 for batters (n=94) and 0.18 for bowlers (n=105), or roughly 0.3 after Spearman-Brown
correction. Pressure performance is only partly a stable skill in two-year samples, which is a known property of cricket data.
The method therefore leans on shrinkage and reports uncertainty instead of hiding it; it is a strong filter, not a crystal ball.
Other limitations. No team-strength or pitch data, so the innings-1 win-probability model is coarse and chases dominate
the pressure sample. Batters are credited for bowlers' errors and fielders' catches only through the joint model, not directly.
Death-over specialists with few overs and players seen mainly in one league have wider uncertainty. Players with fewer than
80 pressure deliveries are not ranked, so very young or recently capped names may be under-represented (Vaibhav
Sooryavanshi just misses the batting top 10 at rank 11 despite a small sample).
7. Reproducibility
Pipeline: (1) clean to legal-ball states, runs = bat + extras; (2) out-of-fold win-probability models; (3) leverage by
counterfactual outcomes; (4) high-pressure flag; (5) joint ridge for Impact and Resilience, Beta-shrunk Consistency; (6)
composite, sensitivity, bootstrap, split-half. All thresholds are listed above; scripts accompany this report and use only the
supplied data pack.
